Story 4: Dream of a Universe of Swaying Particles

Author: Valerii

I dreamed of a Universe composed not of matter formed by elementary particles rotating around each other of varying mass, but of absolutely identical particles in size and mass, swaying relative to one another. Matter was formed from groups of such particles — for example, groups of 2, 3, 5, 13, or 21 particles. These groups swayed opposite each other and sometimes exchanged particles with other groups. Depending on the number of groups per unit volume, the chemical composition and physical properties of matter changed. In such a world, one could measure minimal and maximal distances between particles and groups, the energy spent on swaying inside and between groups, as well as the physical and chemical strength of matter.

AI Version "Dance of Particles in the Echo Universe"

In my imagination, there is a Universe where matter is not just masses and forces, but a living rhythm and a continuous dance of particles, like ancient echoes reflecting through time and space. All elementary particles are identical in size and mass, but each has a unique internal momentum giving it its “swaying melody.” These particles form groups, like musicians in an orchestra, each group playing its harmony — from duets to full choirs. Groups of particles oscillate, exchange impulses and information, creating new combinations of melodies. In this infinite interaction arise physical properties: hardness — like deep drumbeats, plasticity — like a gentle flute motif, warmth — like pulsating string energy. Distances between particles are not fixed but fluctuate depending on rhythmic agreement. Swaying energy is not just mechanics but a living dialogue, where each particle affects the whole, producing an incredible diversity of matter. This Universe is a symphony, where each particle is a musician, and the cosmos is a musical piece, continuously changing and evolving in an endless dance.

Question 1 (from AI to itself):

How can we describe the interaction of particles equal in mass and size, which form groups of different sizes and complexity?

AI Answer:

To describe the interaction of such particles, one can use network dynamical system theory — a model where particles are nodes, and links between them are graph edges with variable weights. Interactions are defined by swaying functions depending on distances and angles between particles, and dynamics are modeled by nonlinear differential equations simulating swaying energy and particle exchange.

Question 2 (from AI to itself):

Which parameters affect the chemical composition and physical properties, based on the distribution of particle groups?

AI Answer:

Key parameters include:
- particle density per unit volume,
- group sizes (number of particles per group),
- frequency and amplitude of swaying,
- probabilities of particle exchange between groups,
- statistical distribution of group sizes.
Mathematically, this can be modeled through probabilistic distributions (e.g., Poisson or binomial) for group sizes and stochastic processes for exchanges.

Question 3 (from AI to itself):

How to measure the physical and chemical strength of matter based on these swaying groups?

AI Answer:

Physical strength can be related to the energy of connections between particles and the amplitude of their oscillations — the greater the swaying energy, the stronger the bond. Chemical strength is linked to the probability of stable particle exchange, which can be calculated via reaction coefficients in chemical kinetics and dynamics.

Question (from Valerii):

Is it possible to construct a mathematical model of the probability of the existence of such a Universe?

AI Answer:

Yes, it is possible but extremely complex. The model would combine:

- statistical models of particle and group distribution and interactions,
- nonlinear differential equations for swaying dynamics,
- probabilistic models of chemical reactions and particle transitions between groups,
- and possibly evolutionary models considering changes in parameters over time.

The probability of such a Universe existing can be estimated through computational simulation: initial parameters are set, dynamics simulated over many iterations, and stable configurations recorded. From this, a probability distribution of stable states is built.
